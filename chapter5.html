# Latency trials data

data_latency <- read_excel(here("Chapter 5", "Maze behaviour latency.xlsx"))  %>% 
  mutate(
    # Adjusting treatment level order and names
    Sleep = factor(
      Sleep,
      levels = c("Normal", "Light"),
      labels = c("Normal Light", "Constant Light")
    ),
    Food = factor(
      Food,
      levels = c("Low", "Control", "High"),
#     labels = c("Low Protein", "Control Protein", "High Protein")
    )
  ) %>%
  # Syntax is: New_Name = `Old Name`
  rename(Trial_Average = `Trial Average`) %>%
  # 2. Convert ALL columns that start with "Trial" from dttm into numeric seconds
  mutate(
    across(starts_with("Trial"), 
           ~ hour(.) * 3600 + minute(.) * 60 + second(.))
  ) %>%
  
  # 3. Calculate the row-by-row total for Trial 1 through Trial 10
  rowwise() %>%
  mutate(
    # We use num_range to specifically grab "Trial 1" through "Trial 10" 
    # so we don't accidentally include "Trial_Average" in the sum
    Trial_Total = sum(c_across(num_range("Trial ", 1:10)), na.rm = TRUE)
  ) %>%
  
  # 4. Remove rowwise grouping
  ungroup()

# Create long data format for trial data

data_latency_long <- data_latency %>%
  pivot_longer(
    cols = starts_with("Trial "),
    names_to = "Trial",
    names_prefix = "Trial ",
    values_to = "Time"
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    Trial = as.numeric(Trial),
    # If they did not find food in 150 seconds (ie blank cells in original data), it is right-censored (1)
    Censored = if_else(is.na(Time), 1, 0),
    # Replace the blank NA cells with the 150 second cutoff
    Time = if_else(is.na(Time), 150, Time)
  )

# 1. Create composite grouping factors for model specification
# This creates a single column with levels like "Female.Low.Normal Light"
data_agedependent$Group_Full <- interaction(data_agedependent$Sex, data_agedependent$Food, data_agedependent$Sleep)

# Create a reduced group that drops Sex for comparison
data_agedependent$Group_noSex <- interaction(data_agedependent$Food, data_agedependent$Sleep)

# 2. Fit the Full Interaction Model
# We include the parametric term (Group_Full) for the intercepts
# and the smooth term s(Age) for the curves, varying by group
model_distance_full <- gam(
  Distance ~ Group_Full + s(Age, by = Group_Full, k = 4), 
  data = data_agedependent, 
  method = "REML" 
)

# 3. Fit a Reduced Model (Testing the overall Sex effect)
# Here we add Sex as a simple fixed effect (intercept shift), 
# but the shape of the curves only depends on Food and Sleep
model_distance_reduced_sex <- gam(
  Distance ~ Sex + Group_noSex + s(Age, by = Group_noSex, k = 4), 
  data = data_agedependent, 
  method = "REML"
)

newdata_grid <- expand.grid(

Interpretation of linear learning results:

  1. Do They Learn at Week 2? (The Experience Terms)

The short answer is no.The main effect for Experience is completely non-significant (\(\text{Estimate} = 0.058\), \(p = 0.409\)).All the interaction terms with experience (Experience:Food, Experience:Sleep, Experience:Sex) have p-values ranging from 0.560 to 0.938.

What this means biologically: Once a 2-week-old fly manages to find the food for the very first time, its performance on all subsequent trials remains completely flat. They do not get faster or more reliable with practice, and neither sleep deprivation nor dietary restriction alters this flat trajectory.

At week 2, their post-success behavior is highly stable.2.

  1. The Standout Finding: A Strong Sex EffectThe only statistically significant result in your fixed effects is SexMale (\(\text{Estimate} = 0.996\), \(p = 0.027\)).

What this means biologically: Because this coefficient is positive, 2-week-old male flies have significantly higher odds of failing the assay (getting censored) across these trials compared to females. Since none of the interaction terms with Sex are significant, this male performance deficit is a clean main effect that holds true regardless of whether they are on low, control, or high food, or whether they were kept in constant light. At young adulthood, females are simply more reliable foragers after that initial success.

  1. Food and Sleep Don’t Have Main Effects Here At week 2, the baseline differences for FoodControl, FoodHigh, and SleepConstant Light are all non-significant. Any massive drops in performance we see in the earlier full-dataset GAMM are likely driven by the older age groups (weeks 6 and 8) rather than these young, resilient 2-week-old flies.

For Presentation A flat line is still a highly meaningful result. You can frame this slide beautifully around the concept of “Baseline Competence vs. Cognitive Improvement.”The Narrative: You can tell your audience: “We wanted to see if flies actively learn and improve after their first successful foraging experience. At week 2, they don’t. Their post-success performance is flat, suggesting that their initial success captures their maximum baseline competence for this maze shape at a young age.”The Visual Impact: When you run the ggplot code provided earlier with this specific model, your prediction lines across the Experience axis will look like horizontal steps. The lines for males will sit noticeably lower on the “Probability of Success” vertical axis than the lines for females, perfectly visualizing that significant \(p = 0.027\) sex difference.

The Week 8 Warning: Numerical Instability Look at the rows for Age_Factor8 and its interactions. The estimates are huge (e.g., -16.27, 16.69) and the standard errors are astronomical (3437.18).

This is a classic symptom of complete separation or a severe data shortage in your final cohort. Because the experience analysis drops all trials up to and including the first success, you are only modeling flies that found food and had trials left over. By Week 8, due to natural mortality or sheer drop-offs in foraging activity, you likely have almost no flies left in certain treatment combinations.When an emergency happens in the data like this, the optimizer hits a wall, and the Week 8 p-values (all sitting at ~0.996) become completely meaningless.

A positive estimate means that at 6 weeks old, flies have significantly higher odds of failing the assay on subsequent trials compared to 2-week-old flies.
